# Cognitive rhythm and pacing for presentations

## Audience moves

Use slide roles that reflect what the audience's mind must do:

- **Orient**: locate the situation, actors, scope, or map.
- **Tension / Question**: recognize a conflict, anomaly, tradeoff, or unresolved justification.
- **Observe**: inspect data, an example, a quote, or a concrete case.
- **Explain**: understand a mechanism or causal relationship.
- **Reframe**: revise the initial interpretation.
- **Compare**: evaluate options, scenarios, or criteria.
- **Decide**: accept a conclusion or choose.
- **Act**: understand owners, sequence, measures, or next steps.
- **Consolidate**: reconnect the governing message or objective and any applicable ask or next use.

A deck becomes “high-density flat” when every slide only asserts or explains, even when each slide is individually polished.

## Semantic visual rhythm

Map the visual form to the audience operation:

| Audience move | Useful visual forms |
|---|---|
| Orient | map, scope diagram, annotated overview, message-led agenda |
| Tension | contrast, gap, before/after, anomaly chart |
| Observe | focused chart, concrete case, screenshot, quote, example |
| Explain | causal chain, process, system diagram, annotated mechanism |
| Reframe | synthesis slide, changed model, “not X but Y” contrast |
| Compare | criteria table, option matrix, scenario chart |
| Decide | recommendation, decision frame, explicit tradeoff |
| Act | roadmap, owner map, milestones, measurement loop |
| Consolidate | concise recap of the governing message, objective, ask, or next use |

Do not vary layouts merely for novelty. Repetition is useful when the audience is comparing like with like.

## Create attention without hiding the answer

A transition may leave one question open:

- “The recommendation is to standardize intake first. Why, then, did staffing appear to be the bottleneck?”
- “The overall average improved, but did every customer segment benefit?”

For decision decks, show the recommendation early. The suspense belongs in the justification, tradeoff, or implication.

## Density and breathing slides

After a dense evidence slide, deliberately use one of:

- a one-sentence synthesis;
- a concrete example;
- a causal diagram;
- a reframe;
- an option comparison;
- a decision or implication.

A “breathing slide” is not filler. It changes the cognitive operation and consolidates the evidence.

## Time weighting

Assign each core slide a time weight:

- **Low**: orientation, transition, or concise synthesis.
- **Medium**: one explanation, example, or option.
- **High**: complex evidence, tradeoff, decision, or discussion.

Budget by total cognitive weight, not slide count alone. Reserve time for transitions, interaction,
the intended learning, discussion, or decision work, and any applicable ask. Move detail to appendix
rather than shrinking type.

## Live, read-ahead, and hybrid decks

### Live

- Use less visible prose and stronger visual focus.
- Put transitions, examples, and nuance in speaker notes.
- Do not make the slide meaningless without the speaker.

### Read-ahead

- Include enough connective explanation and source context to stand alone.
- Use stronger internal navigation and explicit implications.
- Avoid presentation-only fragments.

### Hybrid

- Keep the core visual and concise.
- Add compact explanatory text, notes, and appendix support without duplicating everything.

## Filmstrip audit

Inspect slide thumbnails and role labels. Investigate:

- three or more consecutive dense Explain/Observe slides with no synthesis or implication;
- repeated equal-weight layouts for unequal ideas;
- every slide using the same card grid;
- abrupt jumps where the presenter must invent the logic;
- high-weight slides clustered without synthesis, application, a checkpoint, or a decision;
- a generic summary that does not reconnect to the governing message, objective, or next use.
